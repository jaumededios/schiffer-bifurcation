A counterexample to Schiffer's conjecture

Schiffer's conjecture asks whether an overdetermined Laplace eigenvalue problem characterizes the disk among smooth bounded planar domains. The disk is locally rigid, whereas the analogous problems on the sphere and flat cylinder admit nontrivial bifurcations. We construct planar counterexamples by studying highly symmetric domains through their cone quotients. Near its boundary, a cone of large radius approaches a half-cylinder. We transfer the cylindrical bifurcation to these cones and recover a planar domain when the real cone parameter reaches an integer.

01

The overdetermined eigenvalue problem

Which domains have the Schiffer property?

A smooth bounded domain \(\Omega\subset\mathbb R^2\) is said to have the Schiffer property if there are \(\lambda>0\) and a nonconstant function \(u\) satisfying (1.1).

The boundary value has been normalized to \(1\). Indeed, if the common boundary value were \(0\), then both the Dirichlet and Neumann data would vanish and uniqueness for the elliptic Cauchy problem would force \(u\equiv0\). Thus any nontrivial solution has a nonzero boundary value, and rescaling \(u\) makes that value equal to \(1\).

The system is overdetermined: a second-order elliptic equation normally prescribes either Dirichlet data or Neumann data, not both. Every disk has infinitely many radial solutions, one for each positive zero of \(J_1\). Schiffer conjectured that disks were the only bounded planar domains with this property. We will construct Schiffer solutions on noncircular domains.

\(\operatorname{Schiffer}(\Omega)\)
\[ \begin{cases} -\Delta u=\lambda u & \text{in }\Omega,\\ u=1 & \text{on }\partial\Omega,\\ \partial_\nu u=0 & \text{on }\partial\Omega. \end{cases} \]
(1.1)

\(\lambda>0\) and \(u\) is not constant.

2.1 · the linearized problem

Differentiating a family of Schiffer domains produces a Dirichlet eigenfunction.

Let \(V_t\) be a smooth time-dependent vector field with flow \(\Phi_t\), and put \(\Omega_t=\Phi_t(\Omega)\). Its normal component \(f=V_0\mathbin{\cdot}\nu\) is the first-order motion of the boundary; the tangential component only changes its parametrization.

Suppose every \(\Omega_t\) carries a solution \((u_t,\lambda_t)\) of (1.1). Proposition 2.1 states the necessary condition obtained by differentiating this genuine family at \(t=0\).

Proposition 2.1 a necessary spectral condition for deformation

A nonradial first-order deformation forces a Dirichlet eigenvalue.

Let \(\Omega\) be rotationally symmetric, and let \((u,\lambda)\) be a radial solution of (1.1). Suppose \(\Omega_t=\Phi_t(\Omega)\) is a smooth family as above and that each \(\Omega_t\) has a Schiffer solution \((u_t,\lambda_t)\) depending smoothly on \(t\), with \((u_0,\lambda_0)=(u,\lambda)\).

Let \(w\) be the shape derivative of \(u_t\), and let \(w_\ell\) and \(f_\ell\) denote the angular Fourier components of \(w\) and \(f=V_0\mathbin{\cdot}\nu\) in a mode \(\ell\ge1\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} -\Delta w_\ell&=\lambda w_\ell && \text{in }\Omega,\\ w_\ell&=0,\qquad \partial_\nu w_\ell=\lambda f_\ell && \text{on }\partial\Omega. \end{aligned} \] (2.1)

If \(f_\ell\not\equiv0\), then \(w_\ell\not\equiv0\). The first two relations in (2.1) therefore say precisely that \(w_\ell\) is a Dirichlet eigenfunction of \(-\Delta\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\). Consequently, if \(\lambda\) is not in the Dirichlet spectrum in mode \(\ell\), the boundary cannot move to first order in that mode.

Thus the absence of a nonradial Dirichlet eigenfunction at \(\lambda\) gives linear stability modulo geometric symmetries. Nonlinear local rigidity requires the additional argument in Proposition 2.2 and Theorem 2.3.

Proof of Proposition 2.1expand +

Proof. Pull the functions back to the fixed domain by writing \(\widehat u_t=u_t\circ\Phi_t\), and define the shape derivative

\[ w=\left.\frac d{dt}\right|_{t=0}\widehat u_t- \nabla u\mathbin{\cdot}V_0. \]

For \(p\in\partial\Omega\), the identity \(u_t(\Phi_t(p))=1\) gives \(\left.\frac d{dt}\right|_0\widehat u_t=0\). Since \(\nabla_{\partial\Omega}u=0\) and \(f=V_0\mathbin{\cdot}\nu\), it follows that

\[ w=-f\,\partial_\nu u=0 \qquad\text{on }\partial\Omega. \]

The shape derivative of the Neumann condition is

\[ \partial_\nu w+f\,\partial_{\nu\nu}u -\nabla_{\partial\Omega}u\mathbin{\cdot} \nabla_{\partial\Omega}f=0. \]

The last term vanishes because \(u=1\) on the boundary. On \(\partial\Omega\),

\[ \Delta u=\partial_{\nu\nu}u+ \kappa\partial_\nu u+\Delta_{\partial\Omega}u =\partial_{\nu\nu}u. \]

Using \(-\Delta u=\lambda u\), \(u=1\), and \(\partial_\nu u=0\), we obtain \(\partial_{\nu\nu}u=-\lambda\), and therefore

\[ \partial_\nu w=\lambda f \qquad\text{on }\partial\Omega. \]

Finally, differentiating the equation in the interior gives

\[ (-\Delta-\lambda)w=\dot\lambda_0u \qquad\text{in }\Omega. \]

Because \(u\) is radial, the right-hand side has zero projection onto every angular mode \(\ell\ge1\). Projecting the three identities above gives (2.1). If \(f_\ell\not\equiv0\), then \(\partial_\nu w_\ell=\lambda f_\ell\) implies \(w_\ell\not\equiv0\); hence \(w_\ell\) is a Dirichlet eigenfunction with eigenvalue \(\lambda\). □

02

The Schiffer property near a disk

Can a Schiffer disk be perturbed?

The argument has two inputs and one conclusion. Proposition 2.1 differentiates a moving Schiffer domain and produces a Dirichlet eigenfunction. Proposition 2.2 solves that linearized problem on the disk: Bourget's hypothesis removes every higher angular mode, leaving only dilation and translation. After center and scale are fixed, the linearized operator is invertible, and local rigidity follows.

2.2 · classification on the disk

On the disk, the linearized equation has only the geometric solutions.

Proposition 2.2 classification of the linearized disk problem

Every solution of the linearized disk problem is a dilation or a translation.

Let \(D\subset\mathbb R^2\) be the unit disk. Fix \(\rho>0\) with \(J_1(\rho)=0\), and set \(u_0(r)=J_0(\rho r)/J_0(\rho)\) and \(\lambda_0=\rho^2\). Suppose \((f,w,\mu)\) satisfies the equations obtained by differentiating (1.1):

\[ \begin{aligned} (-\Delta-\rho^2)w&=\mu u_0 &&\text{in }D,\\ w&=0,\qquad \partial_\nu w=\rho^2f &&\text{on }\partial D. \end{aligned} \]

Then its boundary velocity has the form

\[ f(\theta)=c+a_1\cos\theta+a_2\sin\theta \qquad(c,a_1,a_2\in\mathbb R). \]

Conversely, every such velocity is realized by an exact family obtained by dilating and translating the disk. Thus these are exactly the solutions of the linearized shape problem.

Proof of Proposition 2.2expand +
Radial base solution

Proof. Every radial solution regular at the origin is a multiple of \(J_0(\rho r)\). The normalization \(u_0=1\) on \(r=1\) gives

\[ u_0(r)=\frac{J_0(\rho r)}{J_0(\rho)},\qquad \lambda=\rho^2. \]

The Neumann condition is

\[ \partial_r u_0(1) =-\rho\frac{J_1(\rho)}{J_0(\rho)}=0. \]

Thus \(J_1(\rho)=0\). Interlacing of the zeros of \(J_0\) and \(J_1\) ensures that \(J_0(\rho)\ne0\), so the normalization is well-defined.

Separation of the nonradial modes

Write the \(\ell\)-th Fourier component of the linearized field as

\[ \begin{aligned} w_\ell(r,\theta)&=J_\ell(\rho r)\Phi_\ell(\theta),\\ \Phi_\ell(\theta)&=A_\ell\cos(\ell\theta)+B_\ell\sin(\ell\theta), \qquad \ell\ge1. \end{aligned} \]

Regularity at \(r=0\) excludes \(Y_\ell\). The Dirichlet condition \(w_\ell=0\) at \(r=1\) gives

\[ J_\ell(\rho)A_\ell=J_\ell(\rho)B_\ell=0. \]

If \(J_\ell(\rho)\ne0\), then \(w_\ell=0\), and the boundary equation in (2.1) gives \(f_\ell=0\). Hence a nonzero shape mode of order \(\ell\) requires the common zero \(J_1(\rho)=J_\ell(\rho)=0\).

Bourget–Siegel theorem Bourget's hypothesis, proved by Siegel

Distinct integer-order Bessel functions have no common positive zero.

\[ \begin{gathered} m,n\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0},\qquad m\ne n,\qquad \rho>0,\\ \Longrightarrow\quad \neg\bigl(J_m(\rho)=J_n(\rho)=0\bigr). \end{gathered} \]

Apply the theorem with \(m=1\) and \(n=\ell\). Since the radial Schiffer solution already satisfies \(J_1(\rho)=0\), every angular mode \(\ell\ge2\) vanishes in the linearized shape kernel. The only nonradial modes left are \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\).

References: DLMF §10.21; Watson, A Treatise on the Theory of Bessel Functions, §15.28; Cao-Labora–de Dios Pont.

Translations · \(\ell=1\)

For \(a\in\mathbb R^2\), translation gives an exact family of Schiffer triples:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Omega_t&=D+ta,\\ u_t(x)&=u_0(x-ta),\\ \lambda_t&=\rho^2. \end{aligned} \]

Its normal velocity is

\[ f_a(\theta)=a\cdot(\cos\theta,\sin\theta) =a_1\cos\theta+a_2\sin\theta. \]

Thus the entire \(\ell=1\) kernel is exactly the two-dimensional translation orbit.

Dilations · \(\ell=0\)

For \(c\in\mathbb R\), dilation also gives an exact family:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Omega_t&=(1+ct)D,\\ u_t(x)&=u_0\!\left(\frac{x}{1+ct}\right),\\ \lambda_t&=\frac{\rho^2}{(1+ct)^2}. \end{aligned} \]

The boundary moves with constant normal velocity \(f_c(\theta)=c\). The eigenvalue changes because the Laplacian scales by inverse length squared. Since \(J_0(\rho)\ne0\), the radial Dirichlet operator \(-\Delta-\rho^2\) is invertible; hence this one-dimensional dilation family exhausts the radial solutions of the linearized system.

Conclusion of the proof

The zeroth Fourier mode of \(f\) is dilation; its first harmonics are translations; and the Bourget–Siegel theorem excludes every higher harmonic. Therefore

\[ \mathcal K_{\mathrm{shape}} =\underbrace{\operatorname{span}\{1\}}_{\text{dilation}} \oplus \underbrace{\operatorname{span}\{\cos\theta,\sin\theta\}}_{\text{translations}}, \] \[ \mathcal K_{\mathrm{shape}}\big/ \operatorname{span}\{1,\cos\theta,\sin\theta\} =\{0\}. \]

Rotations contribute no additional normal mode: their velocity is tangent to the circle, so \(f=0\). This proves that the disk is linearly rigid after quotienting by the translation and dilation symmetries, and that there is no other infinitesimal shape deformation from which to bifurcate. □

Theorem 2.3 local rigidity of the Schiffer disk

There are no nontrivial smooth perturbations of a Schiffer disk.

Fix \(0<\alpha<1\) and the radial Schiffer solution \((D,u_0,\rho^2)\) above. In a sufficiently small \(C^{2,\alpha}\) neighborhood of this triple, every Schiffer domain is a translated and dilated disk. Equivalently, after fixing the area and barycenter, the disk is isolated.

Proof. Fixing the area removes the constant boundary mode, and fixing the barycenter removes \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\). Proposition 2.2 says that the normalized linearization has zero kernel. This boundary linearization is elliptic Fredholm of index zero, so it is invertible on the normalized slice. The implicit-function theorem therefore makes the disk the unique nearby normalized solution. Undoing the normalization recovers exactly the dilation and translation families displayed in the proof of Proposition 2.2. □

03

Change the ambient geometry

The cylinder and sphere admit the spectral coincidence excluded on the disk.

Proposition 2.1 tells us what to look for: a radial Schiffer eigenvalue that is also a nonradial Dirichlet eigenvalue. The Bourget–Siegel theorem rules this out on a disk. It does occur on the flat cylinder and on the sphere, and in both geometries the resulting linearized kernel produces genuine nonradial Schiffer branches.

flat cylinder

A radial Neumann mode and a nonradial Dirichlet mode have the same eigenvalue.

On the half-cylinder \(x\le0\), separation of variables gives, for every \(\lambda>1\),

\[ \begin{aligned} u_0(x)&=\cos(\sqrt\lambda\,x),\\ v_1(x,\theta)&=\sin(\sqrt{\lambda-1}\,x)\cos\theta. \end{aligned} \]

At \(x=0\), the first wave has Neumann data and the second has Dirichlet data. Consequently, this spectral coincidence holds for every \(\lambda>1\) on the half-cylinder. For \(1<\lambda<4\), every higher channel \(k\ge2\) is evanescent. Fall, Minlend and Weth obtain the corresponding nonlinear bifurcations on compact flat cylinders; the half-cylinder version used below follows from the same separated equations and weighted-space argument.

Fall–Minlend–Weth, JEMS (2025) ↗
hemisphere

A spherical cap bifurcates in an eight-fold mode.

Cao-Labora and Fernández find a transverse intersection of a zonal Neumann branch and a nonzonal Dirichlet branch. This intersection produces smooth contractible Schiffer domains on the hemisphere.

Cao-Labora–Fernández (2025) ↗
The common mechanism

The flexibility comes from a precise coincidence of special functions. Separation of variables produces a radial or zonal family satisfying the Neumann condition and a nonradial family satisfying the Dirichlet condition. A bifurcation becomes possible when the two families have the same eigenvalue: trigonometric waves on the cylinder, associated Legendre functions on the sphere, and, later, Bessel functions on the cone. The disk is rigid precisely because the corresponding Bessel coincidence is forbidden.

04

From the plane to the limiting geometry

The intuition

\(\mathbb R^2/C_N\)\(g_R=dr^2+(r^2/R^2)d\psi^2\)\(R=N\) only at the planar lift

The quotient of an \(N\)-fold domain approaches a half-cylinder.

We seek a noncircular planar Schiffer domain with \(N\)-fold rotational symmetry, for a large integer \(N\). Quotienting by the rotations and rescaling the angular coordinate to have period \(2\pi\) gives a cone with parameter \(R=N\). As \(N\to\infty\), the distance from the boundary to the cone point tends to infinity, and every fixed boundary collar converges to the half-cylinder \(({-\infty},0]\times S^1\). 05

Structure of the argument

The proof strategy

half-cylinder \(\longrightarrow\) cone real \(R_*\longrightarrow R(s)=N\) planar \(N\)-fold lift

The construction is carried out in five steps.

Subsection 5.1 constructs a bifurcating Schiffer family on the half-cylinder. Subsection 5.2 transfers this construction uniformly to cones of large real order by comparing their Bessel modes with the separated cylinder modes. Subsection 5.3 computes the first nonzero variation of the cone parameter and proves that it decreases by an amount independent of the initial large crossing. Subsection 5.4 follows the resulting cone geometry toward an integral value of the order. Subsection 5.5 supplies common Bessel zeros arbitrarily close above integers, so the uniform decrease reaches an integer and the cone unfolds to a planar domain.

5.1 · the limiting half-cylinder

Uniform bifurcation for \(2\leq\lambda\leq3\).

In this subsection we will show that the cylinder solution can be perturbed independently of the eigenvalue \(\lambda\in[2,3]\): the same interval in the bifurcation parameter \(s\) works for every \(\lambda\) in this range.

5.1.1 · separation of variables

The kernel of the linearized problem is explicit.

Let \(\mathcal C_-=(-\infty,0]\times S^1\) and \(\Delta=\partial_x^2+\partial_\theta^2\). The undeformed Schiffer solution is independent of \(\theta\). By Proposition 2.1, a first-order deformation of the boundary must come from a nonradial Dirichlet solution at the same eigenvalue. Separation of variables gives the two functions below.

the undeformed solution
\[ u_0(x)=\cos(\sqrt\lambda\,x), \qquad \begin{cases} (\Delta+\lambda)u_0=0,\\ u_0(0)=1,\quad \partial_xu_0(0)=0. \end{cases} \]

This is the undeformed Schiffer solution: it is constant on the boundary and has zero normal derivative there.

the linearized deformation
\[ v_1(x,\theta)=\sin(\sqrt{\lambda-1}\,x)\cos\theta, \qquad \begin{cases} (\Delta+\lambda)v_1=0,\\ v_1(0,\theta)=0. \end{cases} \]

This is the nonradial kernel direction. The angular eigenvalue \(1\) leaves the normal frequency \(\sqrt{\lambda-1}\).

Recovering the boundary displacement.

Write \(h_s=s f+O(s^2)\) and \(u_s=u_0+s v+O(s^2)\). Linearizing the two boundary conditions at \(x=0\) gives

\[ v(0,\theta)=0, \qquad \partial_xv(0,\theta)=\lambda f(\theta). \]

Therefore \(f(\theta)=\cos\theta\) corresponds to \(v=\lambda v_1/\sqrt{\lambda-1}\). This is exactly the first-order term in Theorem 5.1.

Existence of the nonlinear branch.

Fall, Minlend and Weth prove the corresponding bifurcation theorem for compact flat cylinders. The half-cylinder statement used here follows from the same separation-of-variables and local-bifurcation argument: the boundary condition at the second end is replaced by boundedness as \(x\to-\infty\). For \(2\leq\lambda\leq3\), the inverses on the complementary Fourier modes are bounded uniformly in \(\lambda\). Fall–Minlend–Weth, JEMS (2025) ↗

Theorem 5.1uniformly for \(2\leq\lambda\leq3\)

Fix \(0<\alpha<1\). There is an \(\varepsilon>0\) and a \(C^3\) family \((\Omega_{\lambda,s},u_{\lambda,s})\), indexed by \((\lambda,s)\in[2,3]\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\), such that \(\Omega_{\lambda,s}=\{(x,\theta):x<h_{\lambda,s}(\theta)\}\) for some \(h_{\lambda,s}\in C^{2,\alpha}(S^1)\), and

\[ \begin{cases} (\Delta+\lambda)u_{\lambda,s}=0 & \text{in }\Omega_{\lambda,s},\\ u_{\lambda,s}=1 & \text{on }\partial\Omega_{\lambda,s},\\ \partial_\nu u_{\lambda,s}=0 & \text{on }\partial\Omega_{\lambda,s}. \end{cases} \]

Use rotation in \(\theta\) to choose the cosine direction, axial translation to impose \(\int_{S^1}h_{\lambda,s}=0\), and the first Fourier coefficient of \(h_{\lambda,s}\) as the branch parameter \(s\). In this gauge, put \(\beta=\sqrt{\lambda-1}\) and \(\alpha_2=\sqrt{4-\lambda}\). Then the boundary and field have the forced expansion

\[ h_{\lambda,s}(\theta) =s\cos\theta-\frac{\alpha_2}{4}s^2\cos2\theta+O(s^3), \] \[\begin{aligned} u_{\lambda,s}(x,\theta)&=\cos(\sqrt\lambda\,x)\\ &\quad+s\frac{\lambda}{\beta}\sin(\beta x)\cos\theta\\ &\quad-\frac{\lambda s^2}{4} \left(\cos(\sqrt\lambda\,x)+e^{\alpha_2x}\cos2\theta\right) +O(s^3). \end{aligned}\]
Something we will need later

The quadratic term \(\cos^2\theta=\tfrac12(1+\cos2\theta)\) produces a second harmonic. Its bounded cylinder profile is \(e^{\sqrt{4-\lambda}\,x}\), so the coefficient of \(\cos2\theta\) in the boundary graph is \(-\tfrac14\sqrt{4-\lambda}\). In the cone calculation, the exponential derivative is replaced by the corresponding Bessel logarithmic derivative, whose large-radius limit is \(\sqrt{4-\lambda}\).

The remainders are uniform in \(\lambda\in[2,3]\) on every fixed boundary collar. For this range of \(\lambda\), the first angular mode is oscillatory and every mode \(k\ge2\) has the bounded profile \(e^{\sqrt{k^2-\lambda}\,x}\) as \(x\to-\infty\).

Derivation of the first- and second-order termsexpand +
Proof of the expansion

Assume the branch supplied by Theorem 5.1 and fix \(\lambda\in[2,3]\). Expanding the equation and the two boundary conditions determines its two-jet as follows.

1 / Taylor ansatz and gauges

Set \(u_0(x)=\cos(\sqrt\lambda\,x)\) and write

\[ \begin{aligned} h_s(\theta)&=s f(\theta)+s^2g(\theta)+O(s^3),\\ u_s(x,\theta)&=u_0(x)+s v(x,\theta)+s^2w(x,\theta)+O(s^3). \end{aligned} \]

We choose the even direction \(f=\cos\theta\). The conditions \(\int h_s=0\) and \([\cos\theta]h_s=s\) remove axial translation and reparametrization of the branch.

2 / First order

Taylor expansion of \(u_s(h_s(\theta),\theta)=1\) and of the Neumann condition gives

\[ v(0,\theta)=0, \qquad \partial_xv(0,\theta)=\lambda f(\theta). \]

Since \((\Delta+\lambda)v=0\), the bounded even solution in the first angular channel is therefore

\[ v(x,\theta)=\frac{\lambda}{\beta}\sin(\beta x)\cos\theta, \qquad \beta=\sqrt{\lambda-1}. \]
3 / Second-order boundary data

The Dirichlet condition at order \(s^2\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} 0&=w(0,\theta)+f\,\partial_xv(0,\theta) +\frac12f^2u_0''(0),\\ w(0,\theta)&=-\frac\lambda2f^2 =-\frac\lambda4(1+\cos2\theta). \end{aligned} \]

For a graph boundary, \(\partial_\nu u=0\) is equivalent to \(u_x-h_s' u_\theta=0\). Here \(u_0'''(0)=v_{xx}(0,\theta)=v_\theta(0,\theta)=0\), so its quadratic part reduces to

\[ \partial_xw(0,\theta)=\lambda g(\theta). \]
4 / Solve the two forced modes

The interior equation is \((\Delta+\lambda)w=0\). Only modes \(0\) and \(2\) occur, and boundedness at the infinite end gives

\[ w(x,\theta)=-\frac\lambda4\cos(\sqrt\lambda\,x) -\frac\lambda4e^{\alpha_2x}\cos2\theta, \qquad \alpha_2=\sqrt{4-\lambda}. \]

The zero-mean gauge removes the sine solution in the radial channel, while the amplitude gauge removes a homogeneous first harmonic. Finally, \(g=\lambda^{-1}w_x(0,\cdot)\), hence

\[ g(\theta)=-\frac{\sqrt{4-\lambda}}4\cos2\theta. \]

03 / where the phase goes

The local radial phase depends on \(R\)

On a fixed collar, the Debye approximation writes the regular Bessel profile as \(A_R(x)\cos(\omega x+\xi(R))\), with \(\xi'(R)=-\arccos(R/\rho)\). Thus \(R\) and the local cylinder phase are locally equivalent parameters. Along the bifurcating branch, both vary quadratically in \(s\).

5.3

The computation along the bifurcating branch

The variation of \(R\) determines the variation of the Debye phase.

Suppose that a real crossing has \(R_*=N+\delta\), where \(N\) is an integer and \(\delta>0\). To prove that the nonlinear branch reaches \(R=N\), one needs a decrease in \(R\) that is uniform as \(R_*\to\infty\). We show that every sufficiently large crossing in the fixed window \(2\le\lambda_*\le3\) admits the same positive lower bound for this decrease.

Subsection 5.2 varied \(r_0\) at fixed \(\lambda\) to display the basis limit. The proof follows a different path: at a crossing \(J_1(\rho)=J_{R_*}(\rho)=0\), it keeps \(\rho\) fixed and solves for the cone radius \(R=R(s)\). We therefore make two computations in order: the nonlinear boundary equations determine \(R''(0)\), and the fixed-\(\rho\) Debye phase converts that radius change into a phase change.

Corollary 5.3uniform decrease of \(R\)

There are constants \(R_{\mathrm{drop}},s_*,\eta_*>0\), independent of the crossing, such that every real-order crossing with

\[ \begin{gathered} R_*\ge R_{\mathrm{drop}},\qquad J_1(\rho)=J_{R_*}(\rho)=0,\\ 2\le\dfrac{\rho^2}{R_*^2}\le3, \end{gathered} \]

has a cone branch defined through \(s=s_*\) for which

\[ R(0)=R_*,\qquad R(s_*)\le R_*-\eta_*. \]

The number \(\eta_*\) may be small, but it is independent of \(R_*\) for all sufficiently large crossings. This is the estimate needed to reach an integer value of \(R\).

01 / compute the variation of \(R\)

The second harmonic determines the sign.

Half-turn symmetry gives \(R(-s)=R(s)\), so \(R'(0)=0\). The quadratic product \(\cos^2\theta=\tfrac12(1+\cos2\theta)\) produces only modes \(0\) and \(2\). If \(d_*=(\partial_\nu j_{\nu,m})|_{\nu=R_*}\), the regular second harmonic and its boundary slope are

\[W_2(t)=-\frac{\rho^2}{4}\frac{J_{2R_*}(\rho t)}{J_{2R_*}(\rho)},\qquad S_2=W_2'(1)=-\frac{\rho^3}{4}\frac{J'_{2R_*}(\rho)}{J_{2R_*}(\rho)}.\]

At cubic order the first-harmonic solvability condition is \(V_1(1)=\rho^2/4-S_2/2=\mu Q(1)\), where \(Q(1)=-\rho d_*\) and \(\mu=R_*^2R''(0)/2\). Solving this scalar equation gives the exact curvature

\[R''(0)=-\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_*}}{4R_*d_*}\left(2+\rho\frac{J'_{2R_*}(\rho)}{J_{2R_*}(\rho)}\right).\]

For large \(R_*\), the order derivative and the mode-2 logarithmic derivative have Debye limits. Hence

\[R''(0)=-\Gamma(\lambda_*)+O(R_*^{-1}),\qquad \Gamma(\lambda)=\frac{\sqrt{(\lambda-1)(4-\lambda)}}{4\arccos(\lambda^{-1/2})}>0.\]
02 / compute the induced phase variation

The phase variation is explicit.

Along this branch \(\rho\) is fixed. The oscillatory Debye phase at the boundary is

\[\xi(R;\rho)=\sqrt{\rho^2-R^2}-R\arccos\!\left(\frac R\rho\right)-\frac\pi4,\qquad \partial_R\xi=-\arccos\!\left(\frac R\rho\right).\]

Thus \(R\mapsto\xi\) is locally linear and invertible. The signed branch parameter enters through an even function,

\[R(s)=R_*+\frac12R''(0)s^2+O(s^4).\]

The chain rule now gives the motion seen in the second graph:

\[\Delta\xi(s)=-\frac12\arccos\!\left(\frac{R_*}{\rho}\right)R''(0)s^2+O(s^4).\]

Because both factors on the right are negative, \(\Delta\xi(s)>0\): the cone radius decreases while the local cylinder phase advances. There is a linear relation between phase and \(R-R_*\), and a quadratic relation between either of them and \(s\).

the mean cone radius · order \(s^2\)

The cosine has zero average. Consequently, the first nonzero variation of the cone parameter \(R\) is the negative quadratic term computed above.

The large-radius basis limit

Bessel modes converge to cylinder modes on fixed boundary collars.

\(x=r-r_0\) \(r_0-5\le r\le r_0\) \(\lambda=2.4\) fixed
Before taking the large-radius limit

The cone problem is defined for real order.

The cone metric \(g_R=dr^2+(r^2/R^2)d\psi^2\) and its regular separated modes \(J_{kR}\) are defined for every real \(R>0\). A planar \(N\)-fold quotient is obtained at an integer value \(R=N\), when the cone is unfolded into the plane.

Theorem 5.2uniform cone bifurcation

There are constants \(R_{\mathrm{unif}},s_*,C_3>0\) such that every sufficiently large real crossing

\[ \begin{gathered} R_*\ge R_{\mathrm{unif}},\qquad J_1(\rho)=J_{R_*}(\rho)=0,\\ 2\le\lambda_*:=\dfrac{\rho^2}{R_*^2}\le3. \end{gathered} \]

has a nontrivial \(C^3\) cone branch \(s\mapsto(R(s),u_s,h_s)\), defined for the same interval \(|s|\le s_*\), with

\[ \begin{gathered} R(0)=R_*,\qquad h_s(\psi)=s\cos\psi+O(s^2),\\ \displaystyle\sup_{|s|\le s_*}|R'''(s)|\le C_3. \end{gathered} \]

The theorem applies to every sufficiently large real crossing. At any individual smaller simple crossing, the usual local bifurcation theorem gives a branch whose interval may depend on that crossing. For large \(R_*\), a fixed boundary collar converges to the half-cylinder of Theorem 5.1, which gives estimates uniform in \(R_*\). The finitely many crossings in a bounded range of \(R_*\) are handled separately.

Let the cone have boundary radius \(r_0\) and metric \(dr^2+(r^2/r_0^2)d\psi^2\). After separating the angular variable, the cone and half-cylinder have the bases below. The assertion is local: in the coordinate \(x=r-r_0\), the coefficients of the cone radial equation converge on every fixed interval to the constant coefficients of the cylinder equation as \(r_0\to\infty\).

01 / half-cylinder · \(x\le0\)

The separated cylinder basis

\[ f_k''+(\lambda-k^2)f_k=0. \] \[ f_k(x)= \begin{cases} A_k\cos(\sqrt{\lambda-k^2}\,x)+B_k\sin(\sqrt{\lambda-k^2}\,x),&k^2<\lambda,\\ C_k e^{\sqrt{k^2-\lambda}\,x},&k^2>\lambda. \end{cases} \]
\[ u_k^{\mathrm{cyl}}(x,\theta)=f_k(x) \{\cos(k\theta),\sin(k\theta)\}. \]

For \(1<\lambda<4\), the channels \(k=0,1\) oscillate. Boundedness as \(x\to-\infty\) leaves exactly one exponential for every \(k\ge2\).

02 / cone · regular at the cone point

The separated cone basis

\[ f_k''+\frac1r f_k'+\left(\lambda-\frac{k^2r_0^2}{r^2}\right)f_k=0, \] \[ f_k(r)=J_{kr_0}(\sqrt\lambda\,r). \]
\[ u_k^{\mathrm{cone}}(r,\psi)=J_{kr_0}(\sqrt\lambda\,r) \{\cos(k\psi),\sin(k\psi)\}. \]

Regularity at \(r=0\) selects \(J_{kr_0}\) and rejects \(Y_{kr_0}\). There is no modified-Bessel replacement: \(I\) and \(K\) solve the equation with the opposite spectral sign.

The limit on a fixed collar

Put \(q=\sqrt\lambda\), \(\omega=\sqrt{\lambda-1}\), and \(\Phi(q)=\sqrt{q^2-1}-\arccos(q^{-1})\). Debye's oscillatory formula gives

\[J_{r_0}(\sqrt\lambda(r_0+x))=A_{r_0}(x)\cos\!\left(r_0\Phi(\sqrt\lambda)-\frac\pi4+\omega x\right)+O(r_0^{-1}).\]

Thus changing \(r_0\) translates the local wave by the explicit phase rate \(d\xi/dr_0=\Phi(\sqrt\lambda)\). For the higher channels, the nonoscillatory Debye formula gives

\[\frac{J_{kr_0}(\sqrt\lambda(r_0+x))}{J_{kr_0}(\sqrt\lambda r_0)}\longrightarrow e^{\sqrt{k^2-\lambda}\,x},\qquad k\ge2.\]

5.5

Near-integer Bessel crossings

The common-zero condition holds arbitrarily close to integer order.

\(J_1(\rho)=J_R(\rho)=0\)\(\{R\}=R-\lfloor R\rfloor\)sample through \(N=200\)

The spectral coincidence. The condition \(J_1(\rho)=0\) gives the radial Schiffer solution on the undeformed cone. The second condition \(J_R(\rho)=0\) says that the first nonradial angular channel is also Dirichlet at the same frequency. Their coincidence gives the one-dimensional kernel from which the cone branch bifurcates.

The role of integrality. The cone branch exists for real \(R\). Its planar \(N\)-fold lift occurs when \(R=N\in\mathbb N\). Subsection 5.3 supplies a uniform decrease \(\eta_*\) in the value of \(R\). We must therefore find a crossing \(R_*=N+\delta\) with \(0<\delta<\eta_*\).

Theorem 5.5 · near-integer crossings

For every \(\delta>0\) and every lower threshold \(N_{\min}\), there are an integer \(N\ge N_{\min}\), a real order \(R_*\), and a frequency \(\rho>0\) such that

\[ \begin{gathered} N<R_*<N+\delta,\\ J_1(\rho)=J_{R_*}(\rho)=0,\\ 2<\dfrac{\rho^2}{R_*^2}<3. \end{gathered} \]

The scatter plot below is a finite numerical sample of these coincidences. The theorem itself comes from aligning the fixed-order McMahon phase and the large-order Debye phase.

Proof sketch of Theorem 5.5expand +

Proof sketch. Put

\[ \Phi(q)=\sqrt{q^2-1}-\arccos(q^{-1}), \qquad \sqrt2<q<\sqrt3. \]

The fixed-order McMahon expansion and the large-order Debye expansion give, in the relevant range,

\[ \begin{aligned} j_{1,n}&=\pi\left(n+\frac14\right)+o(1),\\ N\Phi\!\left(\frac{j_{N,m}}N\right) &=\pi\left(m-\frac14\right)+o(1). \end{aligned} \]

Choose \(q_*\in(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)\) such that \(1\), \(q_*/\pi\), and \(\Phi(q_*)/\pi\) are linearly independent over \(\mathbb Q\). Kronecker's theorem makes the orbit

\[ N\longmapsto \left(N\frac{q_*}{\pi}, N\frac{\Phi(q_*)}{\pi}\right) \pmod{\mathbb Z^2} \]

dense in the two-torus. Hence, for arbitrarily large integers \(N\), the two phases can be chosen simultaneously so that a zero \(j_{1,n}\) lies just above \(Nq_*\), while a zero \(j_{N,m}\) lies just below it. The errors in the two asymptotic formulae tend to zero, so their gap can be made arbitrarily small.

For fixed \(m\), the map \(\nu\mapsto j_{\nu,m}\) is strictly increasing. Increasing the order continuously from \(N\) therefore produces a unique \(R_*>N\) for which \(j_{R_*,m}=j_{1,n}=:\rho\). Uniform control of the order derivative in this compact \(q\)-window converts the small zero gap into \(0<R_*-N<\delta\); and \(\rho/R_*\to q_*\in(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)\), which gives \(2<\rho^2/R_*^2<3\) for sufficiently large \(N\). □

Computational notes

What is exact, and what is computed numerically.

The limiting problem is posed on the half-infinite cylinder \(\Omega_{\lambda,s}=\{x<h_{\lambda,s}(\theta)\}\). The local bifurcation argument used for compact flat-cylinder domains gives a one-ended branch with \(h_{\lambda,s}=s\cos\theta+O(s^2)\), uniformly for \(2\leq\lambda\leq3\). The finite window is the portion of this infinite-domain solution displayed in the browser.

We set \(\Gamma_s:x=h_s(\theta)=s\cos(\theta-\phi)+h_2\cos 2(\theta-\phi)+h_3\cos 3(\theta-\phi)\). The mean translation is fixed to zero and \(h_1=s\) is the amplitude gauge. The field is a truncated sum of separated solutions of \((\Delta+\lambda)u=0\) in the physical cylinder coordinates.

The \(k=0\) and \(k=1\) radial factors are oscillatory. For \(k\ge2\) they are \(e^{\sqrt{k^2-\lambda}\,x}\cos(k\theta)\) and \(e^{\sqrt{k^2-\lambda}\,x}\sin(k\theta)\), which decay as \(x\to-\infty\). Consequently, the interior PDE residual is identically zero.

For the selected \((\lambda,s)\), a damped Gauss–Newton solve with an analytic boundary Jacobian updates the field coefficients, \(h_2\), and \(h_3\) simultaneously on at least 256 angular samples; a final column-scaled QR solve refines the field at the recovered boundary. Exponential modes are rescaled by constants to avoid overflow without changing their span. The reported defects are evaluated on a separate grid of at least 512 shifted samples in the normalized metric \(\lVert f\rVert^2=(2\pi)^{-1}\int|f(\theta)|^2\,d\theta\). The two boundary corrections and independent \(\lambda,s\) sliders define a finite-dimensional free-boundary approximation. The reported defects measure its truncation error.

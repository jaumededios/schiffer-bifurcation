How do you escape
the disk?

The disk seems spectrally locked. The cylinder can bifurcate. A very long cone lets us carry that flexible local picture back toward the plane—provided its continuously varying angle lands on an integer. We will follow one numerical example all the way to N = 28.

Start with the obstruction ↓
01

The overdetermined question

The disk is the obvious solution. Is it alone?

Schiffer asks whether a bounded planar domain carrying a nonconstant Neumann eigenfunction with one constant boundary value must be a disk. After normalization, the boundary supplies both the value and its normal derivative—Cauchy data for an elliptic equation.

Careful: the disk is an example of the Schiffer property, not a counterexample. A counterexample must be a genuinely noncircular bounded domain with the same data.

The linear test

To move the boundary, two spectra must meet.

At a symmetric solution, a nonradial shape direction can enter the linearized kernel only when the radial Neumann eigenvalue is also a Dirichlet eigenvalue in that angular mode. Without this coincidence, the implicit-function heuristic predicts local isolation after geometric symmetries are removed.

02

Try the linear test on the disk

The only open door is a translation.

radial Neumann stateu(r) = J0(ρr)

Its boundary condition is J1(ρ)=0.

candidate angular kernel ℓJℓ(ρ)=0

The same positive ρ would have to be a zero of two integer-order Bessel functions.

Bourget–Siegel obstruction

No shared positive zeros at distinct integer orders.

Every ℓ≥2 is excluded. The surviving ℓ=1 direction merely translates the disk, so it produces no new shape. The direct disk bifurcation is spectrally locked.

03

Change the ambient geometry

Elsewhere, the same problem can move.

The obstruction is not a universal failure of bifurcation. On a flat cylinder and on the sphere, the spectral branches have room to cross. Those examples suggest a strategy: borrow the cylinder’s local flexibility, but recover a bounded planar object at the end.

flat cylinder

A propagating first mode, then decaying higher modes.

For 1<λ<4, the first angular channel oscillates while every k≥2 channel is evanescent. This is the flexible model we can compute explicitly.

sphere

Curvature separates the spectral branches.

Schiffer-type bifurcations also occur in spherical geometry. The lesson is conceptual: once scaling no longer locks every eigenvalue together, nontrivial crossings can appear.

03 / where the phase goes

Phase freedom becomes real order

Debye turns the tip-regular Bessel profile into AR(x) cos(ωx + ξ(R)) on the collar, with ξ′(R) = −acos(R/ρ). Thus real order and cylinder phase are locally equivalent parameters; along the branch, both change quadratically in s.

07

The missing degree of freedom

On the cylinder it is phase. On the cone it is R.

The cylinder’s critical radial channel has two Cauchy directions, so A cos(ωx)+B sin(ωx) is a wave with a freely changing phase. Tip regularity leaves only one cone profile, JR. Debye restores the missing coordinate: changing the real order R changes the phase seen in the rim collar.

01 / cylinder-limit curvature

The branch bends toward smaller cones.

The leading coefficient comes from the flat-cylinder collar calculation. The negative sign is the mechanism we need: a branch starting just above an integer moves down toward it.

Numerical method

Exact inside, numerical on the wall.

We set Γs: x = hs(θ) = s cos(θ − φ) + h₂ cos 2(θ − φ) + h₃ cos 3(θ − φ). The mean translation is fixed to zero and h₁=s is the amplitude gauge. The field is a truncated sum of separated solutions of (Δ + λ)u = 0 in the physical cylinder coordinates.

The k = 0 and k = 1 radial factors are oscillatory. For k ≥ 2 they are e√(k²−λ)x cos(kθ) and e√(k²−λ)x sin(kθ), which decay as x → −∞. Consequently, the interior PDE residual is identically zero.

For the selected (λ,s), a damped Gauss–Newton solve with an analytic wall Jacobian updates the field coefficients, h₂, and h₃ simultaneously on at least 256 angular samples; a final column-scaled QR solve polishes the field at the recovered wall. Exponential modes are rescaled by constants to avoid overflow without changing their span. The reported defects are evaluated on a separate grid of at least 512 shifted samples in the normalized metric ||f||² = (2π)⁻¹∫|f(θ)|²dθ. With only two wall corrections and independent λ,s sliders, the result is a truncated free-boundary solve rather than an exact branch, so the defects need not vanish.

Cone continuation

The cone panel uses the metric dr² + (r²/R²)dψ². At fixed ρ = j1,16, the field and boundary are expanded in 13 even Fourier–Bessel modes. An offline nonlinear least-squares continuation enforces u = 1 and the exact metric normal derivative on 112 collocation angles; the displayed defects come from 512 shifted validation angles.

The browser interpolates those solved branch records and precomputed radial Bessel tables. At the final record R = 28, the quotient angle 2π/R tiles the plane exactly 28 times. The unfolded view draws the true seam angle and also includes a magnified inset because the initial mismatch is only about 0.339°.

Angular modes

The nested-zoom panel evaluates the whole 28-copy assembly and its seam-centered one-wavelength collar from one interpolated branch record. Its field samples come from the precomputed normalized tables JkR(ρ*r/R); the free rim uses the same continued Fourier coefficients. The apparent flatness of the zoom is therefore a coordinate zoom of the cone data, not a separately drawn cylinder texture.

The radial trace is an additional local comparison, meant as the geometric intuition rather than a quoted step of the proof. Writing r = R + x, the k = 1 Bessel equation tends on bounded collars to C″ + (λ − 1)C = 0. Debye refines this to JR(√λ(R+x)) ≈ AR(x) cos(ωx + ξ(R)), where ξ(R) = √(ρ²−R²) − R acos(R/ρ) − π/4 and therefore ξ′(R) = −acos(R/ρ). The graph anchors the Debye phase at the exact zero R* and also reports the phase extracted from the exact rim Cauchy data; their close agreement is the numerical check of the analogy.

Debye approximations

The bottom laboratory compares radial functions rather than fitted fields. At x=r−R, the exact tabulated profiles JkR(ρr/R) are rim-normalized and compared with the limiting cylinder ODE f″+(λ−k²)f=0. The k=1 curve is Cauchy-normalized and compared with its anchored Debye phase; the k=2,3 curves are divided by their rim values and compared with e√(k²−λ)x.

The critical k=1 curve interpolates the dense evaluated profiles stored along the branch. The k=2,3 curves interpolate their precomputed endpoint Bessel tables over the very short interval R∈[28,28.026397…]. Tightening the collar is the numerical test of the local asymptotic claim: the discrepancies shrink as x→0.

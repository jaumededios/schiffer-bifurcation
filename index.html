A counterexample to Schiffer's conjecture

Schiffer's conjecture asks whether an overdetermined Laplace eigenvalue problem characterizes the disk among smooth bounded planar domains. The disk is locally rigid, whereas analogous problems on the sphere and flat cylinder admit nontrivial bifurcations. We explain how the cylinder mechanism can be transferred back to the plane: a highly symmetric planar quotient is a long cone, the geometry near its boundary approaches a half-cylinder, and a cone bifurcation can be unfolded when its real symmetry parameter reaches an integer.

01

The overdetermined eigenvalue problem

Which domains have the Schiffer property?

A smooth bounded domain \(\Omega\subset\mathbb R^2\) is said to have the Schiffer property if there are \(\lambda>0\) and a nonconstant function \(u\) satisfying (1.1).

The boundary value has been normalized to \(1\). Indeed, if the common boundary value were \(0\), then both the Dirichlet and Neumann data would vanish and uniqueness for the elliptic Cauchy problem would force \(u\equiv0\). Thus any nontrivial solution has a nonzero boundary value, and rescaling \(u\) makes that value equal to \(1\).

The system is overdetermined: a second-order elliptic equation normally prescribes either Dirichlet data or Neumann data, not both. Every disk has infinitely many radial solutions, one for each positive zero of \(J_1\). Schiffer conjectured that disks were the only bounded planar domains with this property. We will construct Schiffer solutions on noncircular domains.

\(\operatorname{Schiffer}(\Omega)\)
\[ \begin{cases} -\Delta u=\lambda u & \text{in }\Omega,\\ u=1 & \text{on }\partial\Omega,\\ \partial_\nu u=0 & \text{on }\partial\Omega. \end{cases} \]
(1.1)

\(\lambda>0\) and \(u\) is not constant.

2.1 · the linearized problem

Infinitesimal deformations are controlled by a Dirichlet kernel.

Let \((\Omega_t,u_t,\lambda_t)\) be a \(C^1\) family satisfying (1.1), with \((\Omega_0,u_0,\lambda_0)=(\Omega,u,\lambda)\). Write a moving boundary point as \(p_t=p+t f(p)\nu(p)+O(t^2)\), and set \(v=\left.\frac d{dt}\right|_{t=0}u_t\), \(\mu=\left.\frac d{dt}\right|_{t=0}\lambda_t\). We call \((f,v,\mu)\) the infinitesimal deformation of the family.

The scalar \(f\) is the normal velocity of the boundary. A tangential velocity changes only the parametrization, not the domain. When the base solution is radial, the nonzero angular modes of this first-order problem have a particularly simple description.

\[ \begin{cases} (-\Delta-\lambda)v=0 & \text{in }\Omega,\\ v=0 & \text{on }\partial\Omega,\\ \partial_\nu v=\lambda f & \text{on }\partial\Omega. \end{cases} \] (2.1)
Theorem 2.1 · linearized kernel

Assume that \(\Omega\) is rotationally symmetric and that \(u\) is radial. In every angular mode \(\ell\ge1\), the linearized shape kernel consists precisely of the pairs \((f_\ell,v_\ell)\) satisfying (2.1).

Projection onto \(v_\ell\) therefore identifies this kernel with the Dirichlet eigenspace of \(-\Delta\) at eigenvalue \(\lambda\) in mode \(\ell\); the boundary velocity is recovered by \(f_\ell=\lambda^{-1}\partial_\nu v_\ell\).

If \(\lambda\) is not a Dirichlet eigenvalue in that angular mode, then \(v_\ell=0\) and hence \(f_\ell=0\). Absence of a common eigenvalue is exactly the first-order obstruction to deforming the domain in that mode.

This is what we mean by linear stability: after the symmetry directions are removed, the kernel of the linearized problem is zero. It is a first-order statement and does not, by itself, prove nonlinear local rigidity.

Proof of Theorem 2.1expand +

Write a moving boundary point as \(p_t=p+t f(p)\nu(p)+O(t^2)\). Differentiating \(u_t(p_t)=1\) gives \(v+f\partial_\nu u=0\) on \(\partial\Omega\). Since the base solution satisfies \(\partial_\nu u=0\), we obtain \(v=0\).

Differentiating the Neumann condition gives \(\partial_\nu v+f\partial_{\nu\nu}u=0\). Since \(u=1\) and \(\partial_\nu u=0\) on the boundary, all tangential derivatives vanish there and the PDE yields \(\partial_{\nu\nu}u=-\lambda\). Therefore \(\partial_\nu v=\lambda f\).

In the interior, differentiation gives \((-\Delta-\lambda)v=\mu u\). On a rotationally symmetric base, \(u\) is radial; projecting onto any nonzero angular mode removes the right-hand side and gives the homogeneous Dirichlet eigenvalue equation above.

02

The Schiffer property near a disk

Can a Schiffer disk be perturbed?

This section gives a first-order reason that Schiffer's conjecture is plausible. If a disk belonged to a genuinely new family of Schiffer domains, the derivative of that family would produce a Dirichlet eigenfunction at exactly the eigenvalue of the radial Schiffer solution. We first derive that necessary condition. We then show that on the disk its only solutions come from moving within the familiar family of translated and dilated disks.

2.2 · the disk obstruction

The disk has no genuine infinitesimal shape direction.

Theorem 2.2 infinitesimal rigidity of a Schiffer disk

The only first-order deformations of a Schiffer disk are translations and dilations.

Let \(D\subset\mathbb R^2\) be the unit disk. Fix \(\rho>0\) with \(J_1(\rho)=0\), and set \(u_0(r)=J_0(\rho r)/J_0(\rho)\) and \(\lambda_0=\rho^2\). Suppose \((\Omega_t,u_t,\lambda_t)\) is a \(C^1\) family of solutions of (1.1) with \((\Omega_0,u_0,\lambda_0)=(D,u_0,\rho^2)\). If \(f\) is the normal velocity of \(\partial\Omega_t\) at \(t=0\), then

\[ f(\theta)=c+a_1\cos\theta+a_2\sin\theta \qquad(c,a_1,a_2\in\mathbb R). \]

Conversely, every such velocity is realized by an exact family obtained by dilating and translating the disk. Hence the linearized shape kernel, after quotienting by translations and dilations, is zero.

Proof of Theorem 2.2expand +
Proof · the radial base solution

Every radial solution regular at the origin is a multiple of \(J_0(\rho r)\). The normalization \(u_0=1\) on \(r=1\) gives

\[ u_0(r)=\frac{J_0(\rho r)}{J_0(\rho)},\qquad \lambda=\rho^2. \]

The Neumann condition is

\[ \partial_r u_0(1) =-\rho\frac{J_1(\rho)}{J_0(\rho)}=0. \]

Thus \(J_1(\rho)=0\). Interlacing of the zeros of \(J_0\) and \(J_1\) ensures that \(J_0(\rho)\ne0\), so the normalization is well-defined.

Proof · separation of the nonradial modes

Write the \(\ell\)-th Fourier component of the linearized field as

\[ \begin{aligned} v_\ell(r,\theta)&=J_\ell(\rho r)\Phi_\ell(\theta),\\ \Phi_\ell(\theta)&=A_\ell\cos(\ell\theta)+B_\ell\sin(\ell\theta), \qquad \ell\ge1. \end{aligned} \]

Regularity at \(r=0\) excludes \(Y_\ell\). The Dirichlet condition \(v_\ell=0\) at \(r=1\) gives

\[ J_\ell(\rho)A_\ell=J_\ell(\rho)B_\ell=0. \]

If \(J_\ell(\rho)\ne0\), then \(v_\ell=0\), and the last line of (2.1) gives \(f_\ell=0\). Hence a nonzero shape mode of order \(\ell\) requires the common zero \(J_1(\rho)=J_\ell(\rho)=0\).

Theorem 2.3 Bourget's hypothesis, proved by Siegel

Distinct integer-order Bessel functions have no common positive zero.

\[ \begin{gathered} m,n\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0},\qquad m\ne n,\qquad \rho>0,\\ \Longrightarrow\quad \neg\bigl(J_m(\rho)=J_n(\rho)=0\bigr). \end{gathered} \]

Apply the theorem with \(m=1\) and \(n=\ell\). Since the radial Schiffer solution already satisfies \(J_1(\rho)=0\), every angular mode \(\ell\ge2\) vanishes in the linearized shape kernel. The only nonradial modes left are \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\).

References: DLMF §10.21; Watson, A Treatise on the Theory of Bessel Functions, §15.28; Cao-Labora–de Dios Pont.

Translations · \(\ell=1\)

For \(a\in\mathbb R^2\), translation gives an exact family of Schiffer triples:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Omega_t&=D+ta,\\ u_t(x)&=u_0(x-ta),\\ \lambda_t&=\rho^2. \end{aligned} \]

Its normal velocity is

\[ f_a(\theta)=a\cdot(\cos\theta,\sin\theta) =a_1\cos\theta+a_2\sin\theta. \]

Thus the entire \(\ell=1\) kernel is exactly the two-dimensional translation orbit.

Dilations · \(\ell=0\)

For \(c\in\mathbb R\), dilation also gives an exact family:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Omega_t&=(1+ct)D,\\ u_t(x)&=u_0\!\left(\frac{x}{1+ct}\right),\\ \lambda_t&=\frac{\rho^2}{(1+ct)^2}. \end{aligned} \]

The boundary moves with constant normal velocity \(f_c(\theta)=c\). The eigenvalue changes because the Laplacian scales by inverse length squared. Thus the radial shape direction is exactly dilation.

Conclusion of the proof

The zeroth Fourier mode of \(f\) is dilation; its first harmonics are translations; and Theorem 2.3 kills every higher harmonic. Therefore

\[ \mathcal K_{\mathrm{shape}} =\underbrace{\operatorname{span}\{1\}}_{\text{dilation}} \oplus \underbrace{\operatorname{span}\{\cos\theta,\sin\theta\}}_{\text{translations}}, \] \[ \mathcal K_{\mathrm{shape}}\big/ \operatorname{span}\{1,\cos\theta,\sin\theta\} =\{0\}. \]

Rotations contribute no additional normal mode: their velocity is tangent to the circle, so \(f=0\). This proves that the disk is linearly rigid after quotienting by the translation and dilation symmetries, and that there is no other infinitesimal shape deformation from which to bifurcate.

03

Change the ambient geometry

The cylinder and sphere have the missing Dirichlet mode.

Theorem 2.1 tells us what to look for: a radial Schiffer eigenvalue that is also a nonradial Dirichlet eigenvalue. Bourget's theorem rules this out on a disk. It does occur on the flat cylinder and on the sphere, and in both geometries the resulting linearized kernel produces genuine nonradial Schiffer branches.

flat cylinder

The radial Neumann wave and a nonradial Dirichlet wave coexist.

On the half-cylinder \(x\le0\), separation of variables gives, for every \(\lambda>1\),

\[ \begin{aligned} u_0(x)&=\cos(\sqrt\lambda\,x),\\ v_1(x,\theta)&=\sin(\sqrt{\lambda-1}\,x)\cos\theta. \end{aligned} \]

At \(x=0\), the first wave has Neumann data and the second has Dirichlet data. Thus the coincidence excluded on the disk is automatic here: the two conditions coexist along an interval of \(\lambda\), rather than at a common Bessel zero. For \(1<\lambda<4\), every higher channel \(k\ge2\) is evanescent. Fall, Minlend and Weth obtain the corresponding nonlinear bifurcations on compact flat cylinders; the half-cylinder version used below follows from the same separated equations and weighted-space argument.

From the plane to the limiting geometry

Extracting a half-cylinder from an \(N\)-fold planar domain.

\(\mathbb R^2/C_N\)\(g_R=dr^2+(r^2/R^2)d\psi^2\)\(R=N\) only at the planar lift

Suppose that a planar field has \(N\)-fold rotational symmetry, with \(N\) large. Magnify one angular period, retain that sector, and identify its two radial sides. After rescaling so that the quotient boundary has circumference \(2\pi\), the same sector is a cone of length \(R=N\). Increasing \(N\) does not open the cone: it sends the tip to the left. Consequently every fixed collar of the rim converges to the half-cylinder \(({-\infty},0]\times S^1\). The limiting problem

The half-cylinder has a uniformly controlled bifurcating branch.

\(\Omega_{\lambda,s}=\{x<h_{\lambda,s}(\theta)\}\) \((x,\theta)\in\mathbb R\times S^1\) half-cylinder
Theoremuniform half-cylinder branch

Fix \(0<\alpha<1\) and a compact interval \(K\Subset(1,4)\). There is an \(\varepsilon_K>0\) and a \(C^3\) family \((\Omega_{\lambda,s},u_{\lambda,s})\), defined for \(\lambda\in K\) and \(|s|<\varepsilon_K\), such that

\[ \begin{cases} (\Delta+\lambda)u_{\lambda,s}=0 & \text{in }\Omega_{\lambda,s},\\ u_{\lambda,s}=1 & \text{on }\partial\Omega_{\lambda,s},\\ \partial_\nu u_{\lambda,s}=0 & \text{on }\partial\Omega_{\lambda,s}. \end{cases} \]

The boundary and field begin with the explicit first angular mode

\[h_{\lambda,s}(\theta)=s\cos\theta+O_K(s^2),\] \[\begin{aligned} u_{\lambda,s}(x,\theta)&=\cos(\sqrt\lambda\,x)\\ &\quad+s\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{\lambda-1}}\sin(\sqrt{\lambda-1}\,x)\cos\theta+O_K(s^2). \end{aligned}\]

The remainders are uniform for \(\lambda\in K\) on every fixed boundary collar. Modes \(k\ge2\) are selected by their bounded tails \(e^{\sqrt{k^2-\lambda}\,x}\) as \(x\to-\infty\). Fall–Minlend–Weth formulate their result for compact domains in the flat cylinder; this one-ended half-cylinder version follows from the same separation, weighted-space, and local bifurcation argument. The exclusion of \(\lambda=1,4\) is exactly where the first oscillatory channel or the second evanescent channel degenerates.

What the theorem says. The size of the allowed perturbation can be chosen uniformly while \(\lambda\) stays away from the thresholds \(1\) and \(4\). This uniformity is what can later survive on sufficiently long cones.

07

The computation along the bifurcating branch

The radius drift determines the Debye phase drift.

Section 6 varied \(r_0\) at fixed \(\lambda\) to display the basis limit. The proof follows a different path: at a crossing \(J_1(\rho)=J_{R_*}(\rho)=0\), it keeps \(\rho\) fixed and solves for the cone radius \(R=R(s)\). We therefore make two computations in order: the nonlinear boundary equations determine \(R''(0)\), and the fixed-\(\rho\) Debye phase converts that radius change into a phase change.

01 / compute the radius drift

The second harmonic determines the sign.

Half-turn symmetry gives \(R(-s)=R(s)\), so \(R'(0)=0\). The quadratic product \(\cos^2\theta=\tfrac12(1+\cos2\theta)\) produces only modes \(0\) and \(2\). If \(d_*=(\partial_\nu j_{\nu,m})|_{\nu=R_*}\), the regular second harmonic and its boundary slope are

\[W_2(t)=-\frac{\rho^2}{4}\frac{J_{2R_*}(\rho t)}{J_{2R_*}(\rho)},\qquad S_2=W_2'(1)=-\frac{\rho^3}{4}\frac{J'_{2R_*}(\rho)}{J_{2R_*}(\rho)}.\]

At cubic order the first-harmonic solvability condition is \(V_1(1)=\rho^2/4-S_2/2=\mu Q(1)\), where \(Q(1)=-\rho d_*\) and \(\mu=R_*^2R''(0)/2\). Solving this scalar equation gives the exact curvature

\[R''(0)=-\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_*}}{4R_*d_*}\left(2+\rho\frac{J'_{2R_*}(\rho)}{J_{2R_*}(\rho)}\right).\]

For large \(R_*\), the order derivative and the mode-2 logarithmic derivative have Debye limits. Hence

\[R''(0)=-\Gamma(\lambda_*)+O(R_*^{-1}),\qquad \Gamma(\lambda)=\frac{\sqrt{(\lambda-1)(4-\lambda)}}{4\arccos(\lambda^{-1/2})}>0.\]
02 / convert radius drift into phase drift

The phase moves by an explicit amount.

Along this branch \(\rho\) is fixed. The oscillatory Debye phase at the rim is

\[\xi(R;\rho)=\sqrt{\rho^2-R^2}-R\arccos\!\left(\frac R\rho\right)-\frac\pi4,\qquad \partial_R\xi=-\arccos\!\left(\frac R\rho\right).\]

Thus \(R\mapsto\xi\) is locally linear and invertible. But the signed branch parameter enters through an even function,

\[R(s)=R_*+\frac12R''(0)s^2+O(s^4).\]

The chain rule now gives the motion seen in the second graph:

\[\Delta\xi(s)=-\frac12\arccos\!\left(\frac{R_*}{\rho}\right)R''(0)s^2+O(s^4).\]

Because both factors on the right are negative, \(\Delta\xi(s)>0\): the cone radius decreases while the local cylinder phase advances. There is a linear relation between phase and \(R-R_*\), and a quadratic relation between either of them and \(s\).

the visible boundary wave · order \(s\)
\[r_{\partial}(\psi;s)=R(s)-s\cos\psi+O(s^2).\]

The two opposite points \(\psi=0,\pi\) move in opposite directions at speed one. This is the first-order wiggle visible in the cylinder and cone pictures.

the mean cone radius · order \(s^2\)
\[\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}r_{\partial}(\psi;s)\,d\psi=R_*+\frac12R''(0)s^2+O(s^4).\]

The cosine averages to zero. The parameter that must land on an integer therefore has no linear motion; its first nonzero change is the negative quadratic drift computed above.

The large-radius basis limit

Bessel modes become cylinder modes near the rim.

\(x=r-r_0\) \(r_0-5\le r\le r_0\) \(\lambda=2.4\) fixed

Let the cone have rim radius \(r_0\) and metric \(dr^2+(r^2/r_0^2)d\psi^2\). After separating the angular variable, the cone and half-cylinder have the bases below. The assertion is local: on the fixed coordinate \(x=r-r_0\), the coefficients of the cone radial equation converge to the constant coefficients of the cylinder equation as \(r_0\to\infty\).

01 / half-cylinder · \(x\le0\)

The separated cylinder basis

\[ f_k''+(\lambda-k^2)f_k=0,\qquad u_k(x,\theta)=f_k(x)\{\cos(k\theta),\sin(k\theta)\}. \] \[ f_k(x)= \begin{cases} A_k\cos(\sqrt{\lambda-k^2}\,x)+B_k\sin(\sqrt{\lambda-k^2}\,x),&k^2<\lambda,\\ C_k e^{\sqrt{k^2-\lambda}\,x},&k^2>\lambda. \end{cases} \]

For \(1<\lambda<4\), the channels \(k=0,1\) oscillate. Boundedness as \(x\to-\infty\) leaves exactly one exponential for every \(k\ge2\).

02 / cone · regular at the tip

The separated cone basis

\[ f_k''+\frac1r f_k'+\left(\lambda-\frac{k^2r_0^2}{r^2}\right)f_k=0, \] \[ u_k(r,\psi)=J_{kr_0}(\sqrt\lambda\,r) \{\cos(k\psi),\sin(k\psi)\}. \]

Regularity at \(r=0\) selects \(J_{kr_0}\) and rejects \(Y_{kr_0}\). There is no modified-Bessel replacement: \(I\) and \(K\) solve the equation with the opposite spectral sign.

The limit on a fixed collar

Put \(q=\sqrt\lambda\), \(\omega=\sqrt{\lambda-1}\), and \(\Phi(q)=\sqrt{q^2-1}-\arccos(q^{-1})\). Debye's oscillatory formula gives

\[J_{r_0}(\sqrt\lambda(r_0+x))=A_{r_0}(x)\cos\!\left(r_0\Phi(\sqrt\lambda)-\frac\pi4+\omega x\right)+O(r_0^{-1}).\]

Thus changing \(r_0\) translates the local wave by the explicit phase rate \(d\xi/dr_0=\Phi(\sqrt\lambda)\). For the higher channels, the nonoscillatory Debye formula gives

\[\frac{J_{kr_0}(\sqrt\lambda(r_0+x))}{J_{kr_0}(\sqrt\lambda r_0)}\longrightarrow e^{\sqrt{k^2-\lambda}\,x},\qquad k\ge2.\]

Near-integer Bessel crossings

Common zeros occur arbitrarily close to integer order.

\(J_1(\rho)=J_R(\rho)=0\)\(\{R\}=R-\lfloor R\rfloor\)sample through \(N=200\)

Each dot is a computed common zero \(\rho=j_{1,n}=j_{R,m}\), plotted at \((R,\{R\})\). The horizontal coordinate is logarithmic and the plot stops at \(N=200\): far enough to reveal repeated near-integer returns while keeping the individual crossings legible.

Why near-integer crossings exist
01 / two asymptotic phases
\[j_{1,n}=\pi\left(n+\frac14\right)+o(1),\qquad N\Phi(j_{N,m}/N)=\pi\left(m-\frac14\right)+o(1).\]

McMahon controls the fixed-order zero; Debye controls the zero whose order and argument are both large.

Computational notes

What is exact, and what is computed numerically.

The mathematical limiting problem is posed on the genuine half-infinite cylinder \(\Omega_{\lambda,s}=\{x<h_{\lambda,s}(\theta)\}\). Uniformly for \(\lambda\) in each compact \(K\Subset(1,4)\), the same local bifurcation machinery used for compact flat-cylinder domains gives a one-ended branch with \(h_{\lambda,s}=s\cos\theta+O_K(s^2)\). The finite window below is only how that infinite-domain solution is displayed in the browser.

We set \(\Gamma_s:x=h_s(\theta)=s\cos(\theta-\phi)+h_2\cos 2(\theta-\phi)+h_3\cos 3(\theta-\phi)\). The mean translation is fixed to zero and \(h_1=s\) is the amplitude gauge. The field is a truncated sum of separated solutions of \((\Delta+\lambda)u=0\) in the physical cylinder coordinates.

The \(k=0\) and \(k=1\) radial factors are oscillatory. For \(k\ge2\) they are \(e^{\sqrt{k^2-\lambda}\,x}\cos(k\theta)\) and \(e^{\sqrt{k^2-\lambda}\,x}\sin(k\theta)\), which decay as \(x\to-\infty\). Consequently, the interior PDE residual is identically zero.

For the selected \((\lambda,s)\), a damped Gauss–Newton solve with an analytic wall Jacobian updates the field coefficients, \(h_2\), and \(h_3\) simultaneously on at least 256 angular samples; a final column-scaled QR solve polishes the field at the recovered wall. Exponential modes are rescaled by constants to avoid overflow without changing their span. The reported defects are evaluated on a separate grid of at least 512 shifted samples in the normalized metric \(\lVert f\rVert^2=(2\pi)^{-1}\int|f(\theta)|^2\,d\theta\). With only two wall corrections and independent \(\lambda,s\) sliders, the result is a truncated free-boundary solve rather than an exact branch, so the defects need not vanish.

Cone continuation

The cone panel uses the metric \(dr^2+(r^2/R^2)d\psi^2\). At fixed \(\rho=j_{1,16}\), the field and boundary are expanded in 13 even Fourier–Bessel modes. An offline nonlinear least-squares continuation enforces \(u=1\) and the exact metric normal derivative on 112 collocation angles; the displayed defects come from 512 shifted validation angles.

The browser interpolates those solved branch records and precomputed radial Bessel tables. At the final record \(R=28\), the quotient angle \(2\pi/R\) tiles the plane exactly 28 times. The unfolded view draws the true seam angle and also includes a magnified inset because the initial mismatch is only about 0.339°.

Angular modes

The nested-zoom panel evaluates the whole 28-copy assembly and its seam-centered one-wavelength collar from one interpolated branch record. Its field samples come from the precomputed normalized tables \(J_{kR}(\rho_*r/R)\); the free rim uses the same continued Fourier coefficients. The apparent flatness of the zoom is therefore a coordinate zoom of the cone data, not a separately drawn cylinder texture.

The radial trace is an additional local comparison, meant as the geometric intuition rather than a quoted step of the proof. Writing \(r=R+x\), the \(k=1\) Bessel equation tends on bounded collars to \(C''+(\lambda-1)C=0\). Debye refines this to \(J_R(\sqrt\lambda(R+x))\approx A_R(x)\cos(\omega x+\xi(R))\), where \(\xi(R)=\sqrt{\rho^2-R^2}-R\arccos(R/\rho)-\pi/4\) and therefore \(\xi'(R)=-\arccos(R/\rho)\). The graph anchors the Debye phase at the exact zero \(R_*\) and also reports the phase extracted from the exact rim Cauchy data; their close agreement is the numerical check of the analogy.

Debye approximations

The radial comparison is deliberately separate from the nonlinear branch. It fixes \(\lambda=2.4\), moves the cone rim through \(26\le r_0\le30\), and evaluates exact samples of \(J_{kr_0}(\sqrt\lambda\,r)\) on \(r\in[r_0-5,r_0]\). The browser interpolates precomputed SciPy values in \(r_0\) and \(r\).

For \(k=1\), both curves are normalized by the oscillatory Cauchy amplitude and the cylinder curve is the unique sine/cosine combination with the exact Bessel value and derivative at the rim. For \(k=2,3\), the Bessel traces are rim-normalized and compared with \(e^{\sqrt{k^2-\lambda}\,x}\). All vertical axes are linear and all comparison curves are solid.
